# Riverside: Who Owns the Next Step?

Riverside is a fictional regional health system anchored by a roughly 450-bed hospital.
Its outpatient network reaches well beyond the city into surrounding towns and rural counties, serving a catchment of approximately 500,000 people.
Twenty-seven specialty services receive about 100,000 new outpatient referral episodes annually from affiliated primary-care sites, independent practices, emergency-department follow-up, and other specialties.
Cardiology, gastroenterology, neurology, orthopedics, endocrinology, and pulmonology provide the principal investigations, but the evidence covers the broader network.

## The Starting Problem

Ruth Bennett in patient relations has been hearing the same practical question in different forms: who is supposed to do something next?
A patient calls the referring clinic and learns that the referral was sent.
The central office says the packet is complete.
The specialty office says the referral is awaiting review, or that more clinical information is needed.
The patient still needs an appointment and has already rearranged work to be available for a promised call.

The complaints deserve a response even before the team can explain the whole process.
They do not establish how often each failure occurs, which part of the process causes the most delay, or whether centralization made access worse.

On July 1, 2025, Riverside centralized intake, administrative completeness checks, tracking, and initial patient contact.
Previously, local arrangements varied and staff absences could leave work poorly covered.
Specialty practices retained clinical review and control of appointment availability.
The change was a reasonable attempt to make coordination more dependable.

An administratively complete referral contains the required documents and fields.
That does not mean a specialist has determined that the information and workup support the next clinical step.
It also does not mean that an appointment is available or that a patient can attend the offered time.
Those distinctions matter when offices report that their part is finished.

## What Riverside Wants to Achieve

The primary aim is timely access to appropriate care.
Riverside also needs manageable staff work, reliable records, financial sustainability, and continuity across its network.
These aims can pull in different directions.
An earlier outside appointment may serve a patient well while reducing Riverside's revenue or leaving its tracking record unresolved.

Each office faces its own expectations.
Central staff must keep incoming work moving; referring practices must care for today's patients; and specialty teams must protect scarce appointments and obtain enough clinical information to use them appropriately.
Meeting each local expectation does not guarantee that the patient progresses through the whole process.
Whether those pressures explain particular patterns is a question for investigation.

## Evidence and Boundaries

The initial evidence contains referral orders from January through December 2025, plus inherited open records from late 2024.
Its cutoff is December 31, 2025.
Later extracts extend the evidence through March and June 2026; use them when the investigation reaches those dates.
The initial extract includes booked future appointments where Riverside already knew the date, but it does not reveal their later outcomes.

Separate tables describe referral episodes, recorded activity, appointments, complaints, sampled record reviews, and new-patient appointment capacity.
Earlier activity histories are less complete than later ones.
An absent event does not prove that no work occurred, and an unresolved record does not prove that a patient received no care.
Recent referrals have had less time to reach an outcome than older ones.
